# What If Your IQ Doubled Every Day? — manus


## c01: The morning it started

Seven AM. Your alarm goes off. And for the first time in your life, you're not tired. You're... awake. Really awake.

At breakfast, your little sister Ellie is crying over her math homework. You glance at it. Done. All twenty questions. In your head. Before your toast pops up.

Mom's old puzzle cube is on the shelf. Nobody in this house has ever solved it. You pick it up. Click, click, click. Eleven seconds.

On the bus, you open a history book you were supposed to read last month. You turn the pages like you're flicking through a comic. You remember every word.

Then you do the math on yourself. Something happened to your brain overnight. It got twice as good. And tomorrow morning, it's going to happen again.

What if your IQ doubled, every single day?


## c02: The rules

Here are the rules. Most people have an IQ of around a hundred. Yesterday, that was you.

Today, you're two hundred. Tomorrow, four hundred. Then eight hundred. Every morning at seven, while you sleep, your brain doubles.

How rare is two hundred? On the IQ scale, it should turn up about once in seventy-six billion people. There are only eight billion people alive. You're already off the chart. And tomorrow, the chart won't even go that high.

It's not just knowing more. You learn faster. You think faster. You notice everything. The tiny crack in the ceiling. The fact that your teacher is wearing two different socks.

And you can't stop it. You can't slow it down. Sleep, and it doubles. Stay awake, and it doubles anyway.

And one more rule. It only happens to you. Nobody else on Earth is changing.

Remember that one.


## c03: The fun part

Day one. Math class. The teacher writes a problem on the board that's supposed to take the whole lesson. You put your hand up before she puts the pen down. She checks the answer twice. Then she just sits down. At lunch, you read every book on the library's science shelf. It takes forty minutes. You'd have finished sooner, but the librarian kept staring.

Your best friend Mo stares at you. "Since when are you smart?" "Since about seven this morning."

Day two. Four hundred. You decide to learn Japanese. The people who train American diplomats say it takes about twenty-two hundred hours of class. You learn it in an afternoon, from a cooking show. By dinner, you're correcting the subtitles.

At the table, Ellie asks why the sky is blue. Normally you'd say "because". Tonight, you use a glass of water and Mom's reading lamp, and you make a tiny sunset on the tablecloth. Ellie's mouth falls open. Then she asks why it's blue again, just to see it twice.

That night, Mo dares you to play the chess computer on his laptop on its hardest level. It's the kind that beats world champions. You lose the first game. You win the second. In the third, you tell the computer how it's going to lose, twenty moves before it does.

Day three. Eight hundred. In the year two thousand, a group of mathematicians named seven of the hardest problems in the world. One million dollars for each one you solve. In twenty-six years, only one has ever been solved.

You read about them on the bus to school. By lunch, you've solved one. You type it up on the school computer and post it online, without your name. Just to see what happens.

Mo reads the first line of your proof. Then the second. Then he slowly closes the laptop.

"Okay. You're scaring me now."

You laugh. Because it's the best week of your life.

But you haven't noticed the one thing that's been getting worse every morning.


## c04: The catch

Day four. Sixteen hundred. And you are starving.

You eat breakfast. Then a second breakfast. Then Ellie's breakfast. By lunch, you've eaten more than your whole family. And you're still hungry.

Here's why. Your brain is only about two percent of your body's weight. But it burns about twenty percent of your energy. Even when you're asleep, it's the hungriest thing you own.

Your whole body, sitting still, runs on about eighty watts. Twenty of those go to your brain. That's like a light bulb burning inside your head. All day. Every day.

Now that brain is doing sixteen times the work. Every morning, it needs more fuel. And everything that burns fuel makes heat.

There's a second catch, too. Talking to people starts to feel like watching a video at quarter speed. You know how every sentence ends before it's halfway out. Mo tells you a joke, and you see the punchline in his face before he says it. You laugh anyway. A second too late. And for the first time in your life, you feel lonely in a room full of people.

Day five. Three thousand two hundred. You wake up with your pillow soaked. Mom puts a hand on your forehead and pulls it back fast. The thermometer says thirty-nine point four.

Doctors say that at forty degrees, your body starts to break down. That's heatstroke. Proteins in your brain begin to stop working. And your temperature is rising a little more every morning.

That's the other problem with doubling. It feels slow, then it isn't. If you keep going like this, by day ten your IQ would be over a hundred thousand. By day thirty, a hundred billion.

But you won't make it to day thirty.

You do the math lying on the bathroom floor, with a bag of frozen peas on your head. At this rate, your brain cooks in three days.


## c05: Someone notices

That afternoon, there's a black car parked outside your house. It's been there since lunch.

Remember the proof you posted online? Without your name? Mathematicians all over the world have been checking it for two days. They can't find a single mistake. On the TV news, a professor holds up a printout of your proof. "Whoever did this," she says, "just changed mathematics forever." And someone traced it to the school computer, at the exact minute you were the only one in the room.

A man in a grey suit knocks on the door. Mom lets him in. He sits at the kitchen table and smiles like he's known you for years.

"We've been looking for someone like you for a very long time."

He offers you a lab. The best doctors in the world, to keep you cool. And every unsolved problem humanity has. Cancer. Clean energy. The things nobody's figured out in a hundred years.

It sounds amazing. Except you're smarter than him now. Much smarter. And you can read people like a book.

You notice his eyes stop on Ellie when he says "family". You notice he says "keep you cool", not "keep you safe". You notice the second man, in the car, who hasn't moved in four hours.

They don't care if your brain cooks. They want everything inside it first. Every answer. Before day eight.

And if you say no? You've already played it forward in your head, a thousand different ways. In most of them, they take you anyway. In some of them, they take Ellie too. Just to see if it runs in the family.

"Take the night," the man says. "We'll be right outside."


## c06: The choice

That night, you can't sleep. Your head is burning, and your brain won't stop.

At three thousand two hundred, you finally see it. The thing that's been changing your brain every morning. And how to switch it off.

But there's a catch. Switching it off doesn't just stop the doubling. It resets you. Back to a hundred. Everything you've learned, gone. You won't even remember how you did it.

Or you can keep going. Go with the man in the suit. Maybe cure a disease or two before day eight. Millions of people could live because of what's in your head right now. And all it would cost is you.

Ellie knocks on your door. She can't sleep either. She sits on the end of your bed in her dinosaur pajamas.

"Are you going away with that man?"

"I don't know."

"Will you still be my brother? If you stop?"

And that's the moment the smartest person who ever lived works out the easiest question in the world.

You grab every notebook in the house. And for six hours, you write. Cures. The proof. Clean energy. Everything you can fit on paper, faster than you've ever written in your life. Ellie falls asleep at your feet.

Outside, the man in the car is watching your window. You see him check his watch at four, and again at five. He's waiting for the morning too. By six thirty, your hand is shaking so hard you can barely hold the pen.

At six fifty-nine, you write the last line.

Then you switch it off.


## c07: The last morning

Seven AM. Your alarm goes off. And you're tired. Normal tired.

Your head is cool. Your mind is quiet. You're a hundred again.

On your desk, there's a stack of notebooks in your own handwriting. You open the first one. You can't understand a single line. On the last page, there's one sentence you can read. "Don't let them have this."

You look out of the window. The black car is gone.

You go down to breakfast. Ellie is already there. She's eating her cereal with one hand. With the other, she's holding Mom's puzzle cube. Click, click, click. Solved.

She looks up at you, puzzled.

"Why does everything feel so easy today?"

It only happens to you. That was the rule. So why is it happening to her?
